🧭 Designing a Life You Actually Want

A working guide to the frameworks psychology and design research offer for figuring out what you want — when "just think about it" hasn't worked.

Why this feels hard: most people try to "find" their life purpose the way you'd look for lost keys — by staring harder. Research suggests the opposite works better: purpose and direction usually emerge from action and reflection, not introspection alone. The frameworks below are tools for generating data about yourself through small experiments, structured reflection, and pattern-spotting — not for having a single epiphany.

Use this as a toolkit, not a checklist. Pick 2–3 that resonate, actually do the exercises, and let a direction form over weeks, not one evening.

Self-Knowledge Frameworks

What are you actually made of — strengths, values, needs?

Values

Schwartz's Theory of Basic Values

A cross-culturally validated map of 10 universal human values (e.g. self-direction, achievement, benevolence, security) that often conflict with each other — meaning your stress may come from pursuing two incompatible values at once.

Do: Rank the 10 values by importance to you, then check which ones your current life actually reflects. The gaps are your friction points.

Schwartz, 1992 — replicated across 80+ countries
Strengths

VIA Character Strengths

Peterson & Seligman identified 24 character strengths (curiosity, persistence, fairness, etc.) that correlate with wellbeing when used regularly — more reliable than "passion" as a starting point since passions are often discovered by using strengths, not the reverse.

Do: Take the free VIA Survey, then redesign one week to deliberately use your top 3 strengths daily.

Peterson & Seligman, 2004; VIA Institute
Motivation

Self-Determination Theory

Deci & Ryan's research shows sustained motivation and wellbeing depend on three psychological needs: autonomy (choice), competence (growth), and relatedness (connection). Directions that starve any one of these tend to feel hollow even when "successful."

Do: Score your current job/life 1–10 on each need. The lowest score usually points to what to redesign first.

Deci & Ryan, 1985–2000, hundreds of replications
Flow

Csikszentmihalyi's Flow

Activities where challenge closely matches skill produce "flow" — deep absorption and intrinsic reward. Tracking when you lose track of time is a more honest signal of real interest than what you think you "should" like.

Do: For 2 weeks, jot down moments you lost track of time. Look for a pattern in the type of activity, not the label.

Csikszentmihalyi, 1990, "Flow"

Future-Orientation Frameworks

Because you can't choose a direction you've never imagined.

Exercise

Best Possible Self

Writing in detail about your best possible future self across life domains (career, relationships, health) for 20 minutes reliably increases optimism and wellbeing in randomized studies — it works better than journaling about the present.

Do: Write 20 minutes, uninterrupted, on "my life in 5 years if everything went as well as it realistically could."

King, 2001, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin
Identity

Possible Selves

Markus & Nurius's research shows we're motivated less by abstract goals than by vivid images of who we could become — including feared selves. Naming both your hoped-for and feared future self clarifies what you're actually steering toward or away from.

Do: Write one paragraph on your "hoped-for self" and one on your "feared self" 10 years out. Compare them to your current trajectory.

Markus & Nurius, 1986, American Psychologist
Design

The Odyssey Plan

From Stanford's "Designing Your Life" course: sketch three different 5-year plans for your life — (1) your current path extended, (2) what you'd do if that path vanished, (3) what you'd do if money/reputation weren't a factor. Comparing plans reveals real preferences.

Do: Draft all three as rough timelines, then rate each for engagement, energy, and coherence — not "correctness."

Burnett & Evans, 2016, "Designing Your Life"
Wellbeing

PERMA Model

Seligman's model of flourishing has 5 measurable pillars: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment. A life goal that only serves one pillar (usually Accomplishment) tends to under-deliver on satisfaction.

Do: Score your current life 1–10 on each pillar. Design your next goal to raise your weakest one.

Seligman, 2011, "Flourish"

Practical / Applied Tools

Less rigorously "proven," but widely used and genuinely useful for structuring the search.

Prototype

Life Design Prototyping

Instead of choosing a life direction analytically, run small, cheap experiments — a short course, an informational interview, a weekend trying a business idea — and let real data (not imagination) tell you if it fits. This is the core method of Design Thinking applied to life.

Do: Pick one Odyssey Plan option and design a 1–2 week "prototype experience" of it this month.

Burnett & Evans, Stanford d.school
Synthesis

Ikigai

The popular 4-circle model (what you love / are good at / the world needs / can be paid for) is a folk framework, not a rigorous theory — but it's a useful synthesis checklist once you've gathered real data from the exercises above, rather than a starting point on its own.

Do: Fill the 4 circles only after you've done the strengths and values exercises — use real findings, not guesses.

Popularized via Okinawan longevity research; conceptual model
Filter

Regret Minimization

A simple decision filter: imagine yourself at 80 looking back — which choice would you regret not trying? Not empirically tested as a framework, but effective at cutting through short-term fear when the "safe" and "true" options diverge.

Do: For any option you're avoiding out of fear, write your 80-year-old self's one-line verdict on it.

Popularized by Jeff Bezos; informal heuristic
Goals

Goal-Setting Theory

Once a direction starts forming, Locke & Latham's decades of research show specific, moderately difficult goals with feedback loops outperform vague ones ("do your best") almost every time — direction-finding and goal execution are different skills.

Do: Once you have a tentative direction, convert it into one specific, time-bound goal with a review checkpoint.

Locke & Latham, 2002, American Psychologist

A 30-Day Way to Use This

A sequence, not a checklist — each step feeds the next.

  1. Days 1–3 — Do the Best Possible Self writing exercise and the VIA Strengths survey.
  2. Days 4–7 — Rank your Schwartz values and score your life on SDT's 3 needs and PERMA's 5 pillars. Note the biggest gaps.
  3. Days 8–10 — Track flow moments daily. Write your hoped-for and feared future selves.
  4. Days 11–14 — Draft your three Odyssey Plans using everything gathered so far.
  5. Days 15–20 — Choose the most energizing Odyssey Plan and design a small, real-world prototype experiment for it.
  6. Days 21–28 — Run the prototype. Journal what it confirmed or ruled out.
  7. Days 29–30 — Fill in an Ikigai check using real findings, then set one specific goal-setting-theory goal for the next 90 days.

Reflection Prompts to Return To

  • What did I do in the last month that made time disappear?
  • Which of my current commitments serves a value I don't actually hold anymore?
  • If I removed the fear of judgment, what would I try for 3 months?
  • Which of my psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) is most starved right now?
  • What would my feared future self tell me to stop doing today?
